The published process behind this topic is simple: share project details, arrange a site inspection, receive a written quote, then have the work completed to Australian Standards. The problem is that step three, the written quote, is where scope can quietly change. One contractor may price preparation, membrane and curing; another may price the membrane only and treat everything else as an exclusion.

The source material is direct on the point: if the answer stays vague, it is hard to compare one contractor against another on anything except price. That is the gap this page addresses. A strong enquiry gives the contractor enough context to decide whether the next step is leak detection, invasive investigation, membrane repair, resealing, replacement or a broader scope. The quote should then confirm which of those paths was chosen, and why.

The Lines a Quote Should Separate

For a meaningful comparison, the useful document itemises the following. If any line is missing, ask for it before accepting the quote.

  • Surface and preparation - which room, deck, wall or slab is affected, and what must be removed or made good before membrane work starts.
  • Membrane system and repair area - the named system, the exact area being treated, and how water paths will be tested.
  • Tiling interface and curing - how the membrane meets the finish, and how long before the area can be used again.
  • Return access requirements - where relevant, who needs the space back, and when.
  • Warranty terms and exclusions - the warranty wording, plus anything the contractor has specifically left out of scope.
  • Compliance reference - the standard or compliance reference where relevant, particularly AS 3740 for wet-area work.

How Surface Type Changes the Quote Structure

The parent material separates several job families, and each one prices differently.

  • Wet areas - bathrooms, laundries, WCs and showers sit in the highest scrutiny group because the source ties this work to AS 3740. The quote should state whether the job is a full waterproofing installation, a membrane reinstatement, resealing, or targeted leak repair after moisture tracing.
  • Balconies and roof decks - exposed tiled balconies, concrete balconies, podium decks, rooftop terraces and flat-roof surfaces can each carry a different scope. Coastal and exposed suburbs such as Glenelg, Henley Beach and Semaphore add salt-laden air, wind and UV pressures on outdoor systems.
  • Below-ground and retaining walls - the source refers to below-ground tanking, sump systems, hydrostatic pressure control, and positive-side and negative-side membrane systems. These are scope categories, not automatic recommendations.

Older Adelaide housing stock is part of the context too. The source describes character homes, original tile beds and renovated wet areas in suburbs such as Norwood, Prospect, North Adelaide, Fullarton and Goodwood. That does not mean every older bathroom has failed. It means the inspection should confirm what is under the tiles and whether the proposed scope deals with the actual leak source rather than only visible grout or sealant.

Asking the Right Questions Before the Quote Is Finalised

The source material recommends asking what will be inspected before the quote is finalised. Those inspection answers belong in the written document, in plain terms.

  1. Can the existing membrane be repaired, or is replacement more appropriate, and how does the selected system suit the surface and exposure?
  2. Will tiles or surface finishes need removal, and how will access be handled?
  3. What does the contractor consider a repair area, and how will the finished membrane be protected?
  4. For below-ground work, does the quote address surface water, wall seepage, tanking, drainage interface or an internal management system?

Because these jobs can hide the leak path, a written quote should spell out the investigation assumptions. If a contractor cannot see the back of a retaining wall, the quote should make clear what is being treated and what remains outside scope. Foothills and sloping-site references support this caution: Magill is described as having larger blocks and sloping sites, while Burnside is linked with seasonal runoff, clay soils, older wet areas and underground spaces.

Who This Applies To

This comparison method is most relevant for homeowners, renovators, strata contacts, property managers and commercial property owners who need waterproofing in a defined area and want a clearer way to brief a contractor. The source states that residential, commercial, strata and managed properties are within scope, so the same method works across a small shower repair, a balcony membrane replacement or a commercial membrane maintenance enquiry.

When moving from reading to quoting, keep the brief short but precise. The best first message names the affected area, property type, suburb or postcode, visible symptoms and any recent renovation or repair history. Note whether the issue follows rain, shower use or general damp conditions, since a stained ceiling below a balcony, a damp wall beside a shower and seepage in a below-ground space may all require different investigation before anyone talks about membranes.

  1. Describe the job precisely. Name the exact room, deck, wall or slab affected, plus the property type, suburb or postcode and any recent renovation history.
  2. Request an itemised written quote. Ask each contractor to separate preparation, membrane system, tiling interface, curing or return access, warranty terms and exclusions.
  3. Check the compliance reference. Confirm the standard or compliance reference where relevant, especially AS 3740 for wet-area work.
  4. Test the investigation assumptions. For hidden leak paths, ask what is being treated and what remains outside scope if areas cannot be inspected.
  5. Compare scope, then price. Evaluate quotes on the detailed scope and warranty wording rather than the headline figure alone.
